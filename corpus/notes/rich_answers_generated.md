# Comparison answers (also shown as tables in the app)

## Key comparisons

### What is the difference between preemptive and non-preemptive scheduling?

Non-preemptive scheduling lets a process keep the CPU until it finishes its burst or blocks; preemptive scheduling lets the operating system take the CPU away mid-burst (on a timer interrupt or a more urgent arrival), which improves response time at the cost of more context switches. Who controls the CPU: Non-preemptive - The running process keeps it until it finishes its burst or blocks for I/O; Preemptive - The operating system can take it away before the burst is finished. When the scheduler runs: Non-preemptive - Only when the running process finishes or blocks; Preemptive - Also on a timer interrupt and when a more urgent process arrives. Examples: Non-preemptive - FCFS, SJF, Priority (non-preemptive version); Preemptive - Round Robin, SRTF, Priority (preemptive version). Response time: Non-preemptive - Can be poor: one long process makes everyone else wait; Preemptive - Better: short or urgent processes get the CPU sooner. Context-switch overhead: Non-preemptive - Low: a switch only happens when a process ends or blocks; Preemptive - Higher: every preemption is a context switch. Starvation: Non-preemptive - FCFS cannot starve a process; SJF and Priority can starve long or low-priority ones; Preemptive - Round Robin cannot starve a process; SRTF and preemptive Priority can. Shared data: Non-preemptive - Simpler: a process is not interrupted in the middle of updating shared data; Preemptive - Needs care: a process can be interrupted while updating shared data, so synchronisation is required. Best suited to: Non-preemptive - Batch systems where low overhead matters; Preemptive - Interactive, time-sharing and real-time systems. Priority scheduling can be either: it is non-preemptive if a higher-priority arrival must wait, preemptive if it takes the CPU at once.

### Compare FCFS, SJF, SRTF, Priority and Round Robin in a table

The algorithms differ in how they pick the next process, whether they preempt, whether they can starve a process, and what they cost: SJF/SRTF minimise average waiting time, Round Robin gives the best response time, FCFS is the simplest, and Priority serves important work first. Picks next: FCFS - Earliest arrival; SJF - Smallest burst; SRTF - Smallest remaining time; Priority - Highest priority (lowest number); Round Robin - Next in a circular queue, one quantum each. Preemptive?: FCFS - No; SJF - No; SRTF - Yes; Priority - Either; Round Robin - Yes. Starvation: FCFS - No; SJF - Long jobs can starve; SRTF - Long jobs can starve; Priority - Low priority can starve (aging fixes it); Round Robin - No. Average waiting time: FCFS - Often long; depends on arrival order; SJF - Minimum among non-preemptive; SRTF - Lowest overall; Priority - Depends on the priorities; Round Robin - Usually higher than SJF. Needs burst times?: FCFS - No; SJF - Yes (or a prediction); SRTF - Yes (or a prediction); Priority - No (needs priorities); Round Robin - No (needs a quantum). Overhead: FCFS - Lowest; SJF - Low; SRTF - Higher; Priority - Low (preemptive: higher); Round Robin - Highest. Best for: FCFS - Simple batch; SJF - Batch with known run times; SRTF - Known bursts, lowest waiting time; Priority - Jobs of differing importance; Round Robin - Interactive time-sharing. Tie-breaks: earlier arrival first, then the lower process number.

### What is the difference between waiting time, turnaround time and response time?

Turnaround time is the whole stay from arrival to completion, waiting time is the part of it spent in the ready queue, and response time is only the wait until the first time on the CPU. Measures: Waiting time - Total time spent in the ready queue; Turnaround time - Total time from arrival to completion; Response time - Time from arrival to the first time on the CPU. Formula: Waiting time - turnaround − burst; Turnaround time - completion − arrival; Response time - first start − arrival. Includes the running time?: Waiting time - No; Turnaround time - Yes; Response time - No. Goal: Waiting time - Minimise; Turnaround time - Minimise; Response time - Minimise. In non-preemptive algorithms: Waiting time - Equals the response time; Turnaround time - Waiting time + burst time; Response time - Equals the waiting time. Relationship: Waiting time - turnaround = waiting + burst; Turnaround time - Always at least the waiting time; Response time - Never larger than the waiting time. Compute turnaround first, then waiting = turnaround − burst; read the first start off the Gantt chart for response.

### What is the difference between the long-term, medium-term and short-term scheduler?

The long-term scheduler decides which jobs enter memory, the medium-term scheduler swaps processes out and back in, and the short-term (CPU) scheduler picks the next ready process to run, very often. Role: Long-term (job) - Admits jobs from the job pool into memory; Medium-term - Swaps processes out of and back into memory; Short-term (CPU) - Chooses which ready process gets the CPU next. How often it runs: Long-term (job) - Rarely (seconds to minutes); Medium-term - Occasionally; Short-term (CPU) - Very often (every few milliseconds). What it controls: Long-term (job) - The degree of multiprogramming; Medium-term - The degree of multiprogramming, by swapping to relieve memory; Short-term (CPU) - Which process runs on the CPU. Speed needed: Long-term (job) - Can be slow; Medium-term - Moderate; Short-term (CPU) - Must be very fast. Algorithms: Long-term (job) - Not the usual exam algorithms; Medium-term - Swapping policy; Short-term (CPU) - FCFS, SJF, SRTF, Priority, Round Robin. FCFS, SJF, Priority and Round Robin are all short-term (CPU) scheduling algorithms.

### What is the difference between CPU-bound and I/O-bound processes?

A CPU-bound process computes for long stretches with few I/O waits, while an I/O-bound process uses the CPU in short bursts between frequent I/O waits. Where it spends its time: CPU-bound - Computing on the CPU; I/O-bound - Waiting for input or output. CPU bursts: CPU-bound - Few and long; I/O-bound - Many and short. I/O bursts: CPU-bound - Few and short; I/O-bound - Many and long. Effect on the CPU: CPU-bound - Keeps the CPU busy; I/O-bound - Often leaves the CPU idle while it waits. Scheduling advice: CPU-bound - Gets a lower priority or a lower queue; I/O-bound - Should be favoured: it needs the CPU only briefly and keeps devices busy. A good scheduler favours I/O-bound processes slightly, which improves overall utilisation.

### What are the CPU scheduling criteria?

Scheduling algorithms are compared on five criteria: two to maximise (utilisation, throughput) and three to minimise (turnaround, waiting, response time). CPU utilisation: Goal - Maximise; Meaning - Percentage of time the CPU is busy running processes. Throughput: Goal - Maximise; Meaning - Number of processes completed per unit of time. Turnaround time: Goal - Minimise; Meaning - Completion time − arrival time. Waiting time: Goal - Minimise; Meaning - Turnaround time − burst time (time in the ready queue). Response time: Goal - Minimise; Meaning - First time on the CPU − arrival time. In exam numericals the comparison is usually on average waiting time and average turnaround time.
